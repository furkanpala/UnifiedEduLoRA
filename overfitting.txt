Overfitting occurs when a model learns the training data so closely that it
captures noise and idiosyncrasies specific to that sample rather than the
underlying statistical pattern. An overfit model achieves low training loss but
fails to generalize, exhibiting high loss on unseen validation or test data. The
gap between training and validation performance is a practical diagnostic for
detecting overfitting.

Model capacity is the primary driver of overfitting risk. High-capacity models
such as deep neural networks with many parameters have enough degrees of freedom
to memorize training labels without learning meaningful features. Reducing
capacity, collecting more data, or applying regularization techniques are the
standard remedies.

L2 regularization, also called weight decay, adds a penalty term to the loss
function proportional to the squared magnitude of all model weights. This
encourages the optimizer to keep weights small, which biases the model toward
smoother decision boundaries and reduces sensitivity to individual training
examples. The strength of the penalty is controlled by a regularization
coefficient lambda, which must be tuned as a hyperparameter.

Dropout is a regularization technique specific to neural networks in which a
random subset of neurons is set to zero during each forward pass of training.
This prevents co-adaptation, a phenomenon where neurons become overly reliant on
specific other neurons and therefore fail to learn independently useful features.
At inference time, dropout is disabled and all neurons are active, but their
outputs are scaled by the dropout probability to maintain the same expected
activation magnitude. The combination of dropout and weight decay is standard
practice in training large networks and tends to produce models that generalize
substantially better than unregularized baselines.
